# Brief for ChatGPT — is time-based heart decay defensible?

**Context.** I'm building "Prove-It," an instrument that scores crypto projects on
delivery, not hype. Each project gets a heart meter (filled hearts / capacity)
plus a timeline graph of that score through time — meter + graph are one
instrument, and the graph (visible rises and falls) is the differentiator.
Scoring rules (v1):

- Capacity tiers {5, 10, 20} by ambition size.
- New projects get a small *unearned* allowance (1–4 hearts) from three
  present-tense checks: working product, team shipping, real economic activity
  (all "last 12 months").
- Promises are written down with evidence criteria *before* judgment; fulfilling
  one earns 0, 1, or 2 hearts.
- **The disputed rule:** after 2 years with no qualifying delivery, the unearned
  allowance drains 1 heart/year. Earned hearts never drain. Abandoning a kept
  promise removes its hearts visibly (rise-and-fall on the graph). The core
  promise gates the final heart.

**My challenge to the decay rule.** It's the only part of the system where the
score moves with nothing happening. Every other change has an event behind it
(delivered → up, abandoned → down). Decay answers "what happened on that date
to lose the heart?" with "nothing, the calendar." The constants (2 years,
1/year) are judgment calls with no principled basis — a critic can fairly call
them arbitrary.

**Proposed alternative.** Kill the timer. Allowance = the present-tense
checklist, re-evaluated each assessment. Project's alive → keeps its allowance;
project's dead → loses it. Every score change ties to an observable, datable
fact instead of a formula.

**Consequences.** BAT 3/10 → 5/10 (Brave is alive: browser works, team ships,
ad buys are real). XRP 2/20 → 4–5/20 (depending on a fresh evidence pass on
bridge-payment volume). A related open question — "do same-lineage upgrades
reset the inactivity clock?" — dissolves, since there's no clock. The graph
stays event-driven: delivered (up), abandoned (down), died (down), revived (up).
Tradeoff, stated plainly: a project that's alive but coasting on ancient
deliveries keeps its score. I'd argue that's correct (the delivery happened;
history shouldn't be rewritten).

**Considered and disliked.** A UI toggle showing the score with/without decay —
doubles the methodology surface and complicates the chart; I hate complicated
charts.

**The question.** Steelman the case *for* keeping time-based decay: is there a
principled (non-arbitrary) basis for it that I'm missing? If not, is the
present-tense alternative the most defensible rule — or is there a third option
that's simpler and harder to challenge?
